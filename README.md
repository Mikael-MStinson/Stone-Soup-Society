# ***The Stone Soup Society***
**Meet your neighbors. Build trust. Find what you need.**

A weekly gathering where neighbors have a chance to play games, get to know one another, discover what they can offer, and find the people and resources they need to pursue their own goals.

### The Story of Stone Soup

> Three monks traveled along a misty mountain road.
> Tired and hungry from their travels, they were now looking for a place to stay and rest.
> When the wind picked up and mist cleared for a moment, their gaze met the rooftops of a village below.
> 
> They could not see from so high above that the village had been through many hard times.
> Bandits and warlords had swept through the village many times, and had seized their goods, burned their fields and left the villagers starved and in destitution.
> Con-men passed through, selling the villagers snake oil products or using feelings of guilt and shame to trick the villagers out of their savings.
> After many years, the villagers had grown weary and untrusting of strangers... and worse, untrusting of each other.
> 
> The villagers worked hard, but they dared not help each other.
> When the monks reached the foot of the mountains, the villagers disappeared into their houses.
> The monks knocked on the doors of houses around the village, but no one ever answered.
>
> "No one will help us," said the youngest monk. "How will we feed ourselves now?"
>
> "Simple," said the eldest. "We will make stone soup."
> 
> The monks then got to work gathering twigs and sticks. They built a small fire in the town square and placed a small tin pot on it.
> When a little girl approached the monks, she asked them, "What are you doing?"
>
> "We are making stone soup," said the middle monk.
>
> "Stone soup? What's that?"
>
> "We will show you," said the eldest monk. "We've got the fire, and the pot of water. Now we just need three large smooth stones."
> 
> The three monks got to work looking for the stones, and the little girl helped.
>
> "Here!" said the little girl. "I found some stones."
>
> "These will do nicely," said the youngest monk, placing them into the pot to cook.
>
> "This small tin pot won't make much, I'm afraid," said the middle monk. "We'd love to make some more for you, but we need a larger pot. I wonder where we can find that."
>
> "My mother has a bigger pot," said the little girl.
>
> She disappeared into her home, and when she returned, she was rolling out a pot big enough to make soup for the whole village.
>
> The villagers noticed this.
>
> "What are you doing?" they began to ask.
>
> "We're making stone soup," the monks would reply. "We've got the fire, and the pot, and the stones. But this broth is bland and needs salt and pepper. I wonder where we can find that."
>
> "I've got some salt and pepper!" said one of the villagers.
>
> "Excellent," said the monks. "I think this broth could use something sweet. Carrots would do. I wonder where we could find those."
>
> "I've got carrots!" said another villager.
> 
> Soon, the villagers were disappearing into their homes again, and reemerging with baskets of vegetables, slabs of meat, bags of rice, noodles, and dumplings, and tossing them into the pot.
> Then, the villagers began to bring tables, bowls, teas and snacks, lanterns, and forms of entertainment like books and puppets.
> Before long, the villagers had put together a feast. They ate and drank until their bellies were full, told stories, sang songs, and celebrated long into the night.
> They had not experienced such a good time like this in a long time. And as the night went on, they began to realize that it could not have happened unless they worked together.
> The villagers thanked the monks for this lesson, and opened their homes to them, offering them comfortable rooms to rest in for the night.
> 
> When the monks resumed their journey the next morning, they said their goodbyes to the villagers.
>
> "Thank you for having us as your guests," the monks said. "You have been most generous."
>
> "Thank you," said the villagers. "With the gifts you have given us, we will always have plenty."


### What We're About

Welcome to the Stone Soup Society.

I want to bring the story you've just read back onto your radar, because I believe it illustrates something important; a value we've largely forgotten.

In this story, the villagers had become afraid to rely on one another. They had learned, through hard experience, that trusting other people could be dangerous.
And when people stop trusting one another, something important disappears.
We can survive on our own. But almost everything that makes life enjoyable - abundance, comfort, and happiness - are only possible when  people work with other people.
None of us can do everything ourselves. We depend on farmers, mechanics, programmers, builders, teachers, artists, doctors, philosophers, bankers, industrialists, scientists, merchants, and countless other people whose work makes our own lives possible.

That's the division of labor.

But division of labor requires trust.
I need to be able to trust the person I'm working with. They need to be able to trust me. And we need some way of discovering what we can offer one another in the first place.

That's where community comes in.
Not necessarily friendship. Not everybody here needs to become best friends.
It's something simpler.

**I need to know enough about the people around me to feel comfortable working with them.**

A market without community is self-consuming. A community without a market is not self-sustaining.

That is what the Stone Soup Society is made to address.

The Stone Soup story gives us an interesting model for doing that.
Notice what the monks actually do.
They have a problem: they're hungry.
They don't simply walk into the village and say, "Somebody feed us."
They take responsibility for solving their own problem.
They start with what they have.
They identify what they're missing.
And then they ask:

**"I'm trying to accomplish this. I have this. I need that. I wonder where I can find it."**

That's a very different way of asking for help.
You're not handing your problem to somebody else.
You're not telling them what they're obligated to do.
You're not asking them to figure out what you need.

**If you ask for a feast, people will guard their pantries.**

Instead, you're simply making your need visible and giving the people around you an opportunity to participate if they want to.
And that distinction is important, because everyone gets to choose.
You can contribute a lot, or a little.
You can contribute your time, your skills, your tools, your knowledge, your connections, your money—or nothing at all.

You can say yes.
You can say no.
You can contribute because you feel like helping. 
You can contribute because you want to trade your contribution for something else.
You can help someone solve their problem, or you can simply enjoy yourself.

That's the whole point.
The goal isn't to turn everyone here into volunteers.
It isn't a business networking group where we exchange business cards and pitch our services.
And it isn't a meeting where everyone is expected to work on the same community project.

**Your goals are your own.**

If you're building a business, build your business.
If you're restoring a car, restore your car.
If you're writing a book, write your book.
If you've got a problem with your computer, fix your computer.
If you've got an idea for something you'd like to see happen, pursue it.
And if there's something you need along the way, this is a place where you can ask:
**"I wonder where I can find that."**

Maybe someone here has what you need.
Maybe they know someone who does.
Maybe they can teach you.
Maybe they can help you figure out how to do it yourself.
And maybe nobody can help.
That's okay too.
Because you're still responsible for your own goal.
The community is simply a resource you can draw upon.

So, how do we build that resource?
The slogan says it all.

**Meet your neighbors.**

We meet regularly because relationships are built through repeated contact. You don't get to know someone by meeting them once.

**Build trust.**

We provide table top games because people who partake in activities together grow more comfortable with each other much faster. 
You could stand around and ask the basic FORD questions, but I can guarentee you'll get to know a person a lot better if you play a game with them.

**Find what you need.**

If you're working toward something and you need a particular skill, resource, connection, or helping hand, bring it up.
Don't ask someone else to take responsibility for your goal.
Just tell us what you're trying to accomplish, what you've got, and what's missing.
Then see what happens.
